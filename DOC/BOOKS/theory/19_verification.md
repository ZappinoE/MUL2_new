# Verification and performance {#sec:verification}

A finite-element program is trusted only to the extent that it has been compared with independent results. This chapter describes the checks built into the test suite and the measurements that show what accuracy and speed to expect.

## Strategy

1. **Patch tests** (exactness on fields the element must represent).
2. **Comparison with a frozen reference program** (the baseline) on golden inputs and on inputs generated by modifying them.
3. **Internal consistency tests**: two different implementations of the same integral give the same matrices; the result does not depend on the number of threads.
4. **Comparison with closed-form solutions** of beam theory.
5. **Benchmarks** at 100 000 DOF for time and memory.
6. **One suite per later feature** (hierarchical expansions, reduced integration, multiphysics, dynamics, buckling, nonlinear statics, curved beams and shells), each against a closed form or an equivalent model, and a complete aircraft as a robustness check.

## Patch tests

A *patch test* checks that an element can reproduce a field it must be able to represent. With $\nu=0$ (so that the section does not need Poisson contraction) a uniform axial strain $\varepsilon_0$, $v=\varepsilon_0y$, must be reproduced exactly by every model, for any distortion of the mesh. The program runs this test for beams (B4, Lagrange section, with and without MITC), plates (Q9 with and without MITC) and solids (H8). The error is at the level of rounding ($10^{-9}$ relative or better).

## Comparison with the baseline program

The frozen program (`MUL2_OPTIMIZED`) has been run on golden inputs (a laminated B4 beam, a Q9 plate, a modal beam) and on inputs obtained by modifying them. The new program reproduces the results as follows.

The differences are explained by the penalty used by the baseline for constraints ($10^{-7}$ in displacements, $4\cdot10^{-6}$ in frequencies) and by two known approximations of the baseline post-processing, which MUL2_NEW intentionally does not copy:

- the baseline uses, at the tying points of MITC, the Jacobian of the evaluation point instead of that of the tying point (the tied shear strains then differ by $\approx1\%$ only when the nodes are not uniformly spaced);
- the baseline produces wrong transverse shear strains for plates without MITC.

## Internal consistency

- **Two kernels.** The separable kernel and the point-by-point kernel give the same stiffness and mass matrices to $10^{-15}$ (dumps of the global matrices compared entry by entry on plate, mixed and solid cases).
- **Thread independence.** The results with one and four threads are *bit-identical*; the assembly scatters in a fixed order.
- **Symmetry.** The element matrices are symmetric by construction; the rigid-body modes of an unconstrained element have zero energy.

## Cantilever beam: comparison between models

A square-section cantilever ($L=1$ m, $0.1\times0.1$ m, $E=70$ GPa, $\nu=0.3$, $\rho=2700$ kg/m³) is clamped at $y=0$ and loaded by $P=1000$ N at the free end. Beam theory gives, with $I=bh^3/12$,

$$
\delta_{EB}=\frac{PL^{3}}{3EI}=5.714\times10^{-4}\ \text{m},\qquad
\delta_{T}=\delta_{EB}+\frac{PL}{\kappa GA}=5.759\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}\quad(\kappa=5/6),
$$

and from {eq:eb-freq} the first bending frequency $f_1=82.25$ Hz. Five B4 elements are used along the axis.

Table: Tip deflection and first bending frequency of the cantilever. {#tab:cantilever}

| Model | DOF | $w_{tip}$ [m] | vs. $\delta_T$ | $f_1$ [Hz] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Euler–Bernoulli / Timoshenko theory | – | $5.714\times10^{-4}$ / $5.759\times10^{-4}$ | – | 82.25 |
| TE1 | 144 | $4.282\times10^{-4}$ | −25.6 % | 94.61 |
| TE2 | 288 | $5.674\times10^{-4}$ | −1.5 % | 82.43 |
| TE4 | 720 | $5.685\times10^{-4}$ | −1.3 % | 82.32 |
| LE, one Q9 section | 1 200 | $5.689\times10^{-4}$ | −1.2 % | 82.33 |
| Plate strip (Q9, thickness LE) | 459 | $5.666\times10^{-4}$ | −1.6 % | 82.48 |
| Solid (H27) | 459 | $5.663\times10^{-4}$ | −1.7 % | 82.53 |

The comments the table suggests are general:

- **TE1 is far too stiff** (−26 %): it suffers from *Poisson locking*. Under bending the axial strain varies linearly over the section and, through Poisson's ratio, so should the transverse strains; with a linear section kinematics those strains are constant over the section, so the transverse deformation cannot follow and the section is artificially stiffened. TE2 is the lowest useful order.
- All models above TE1 agree within about 0.5 % among themselves; their difference from beam theory ($\approx1.3$ %) is the effect of the *three-dimensional clamping*: a perfectly rigid support prevents the Poisson contraction at the root and stiffens the beam slightly, which beam theory neglects.
- The frequencies of the refined models are close to the beam-theory value (82.25 Hz) and approach it from above, as expected for a displacement-based discretisation of a stiffer clamped model.

Two defects found *by* this campaign and corrected are worth recording because the tests that pass now would not have caught them earlier. (i) The first-order Taylor term shared as a Cartesian vector at an edge between shells of different normals suppresses the bending slope at the edge: a thin box came out 5 to 10 times too stiff. The earlier square-tube test passed because its beam-theory reference used a wrong moment of inertia ($\tfrac76$ instead of $\tfrac23$); the reference was corrected and the node rotation introduced (see the section on joining elements in the chapter on curved beams and shells). (ii) The nodal director was the one of the first element listed at the node instead of the average inside the feature angle. The wing of the aircraft, whose stiffness is known in closed form, is the check that exposed both.

### Complete aircraft (analysis 103 and 101)

A model of 14 433 nodes and 129 093 degrees of freedom (3 706 `S9` shells, 4 `CB3` beams, 48 `H8` solids joined by shared nodes with node-dependent kinematics) has exactly six rigid-body modes and a clean spectrum of global modes from $0.89$ Hz (vertical bending of wings and fuselage). The static solution under symmetric wing-tip loads is symmetric to $5\cdot10^{-5}$ (tip displacement $0.33010$ m and $0.33012$ m). The run takes 2 minutes (modes) or 27 seconds (static) on the test machine. There is no closed-form reference for the whole aircraft: it is a robustness and performance demonstration, the accuracy of the parts is established by the tables above.

Two models of about 100 000 DOF were run with the baseline and with MUL2_NEW (Release build, 16 cores, sequential MKL; one run each).

## Known limitations and open points

- H20, T4, T10 and P6 are accepted by the input parser but their shape functions are not implemented: using them gives an explicit error.
- MITC with H8 was reported singular in an early version; the validation campaign found it regular (see the Validation Report).
- Triangular sections (T3) use a one-point rule and are unreliable for beams; use T6 or Q9.
- Very high Taylor orders on small sections are numerically singular unless the section is normalised (Chapter {sec:cuf}).
- Hierarchical expansions exist only for quadrilateral sections and thickness lines (Chapter {sec:hle}); user-defined (`M<N>`) expansions are not available.
- Only point loads are available; distributed loads must be replaced by equivalent nodal point loads.
